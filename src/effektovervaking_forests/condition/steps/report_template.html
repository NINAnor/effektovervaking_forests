Every mapped olivinskog and kalklindeskog stand in Norway is assessed twice: which direction it has moved over nine years, and where it sits today compared with stands that surveyors recorded as being in good condition. The input is the AlphaEarth annual embeddings for 2017–2025 — one 64-number summary of every 10 m cell, every year.

Summary

Both steps measure along the same direction in embedding space, so their two answers can be read together rather than side by side. All mapped stands are scored, one cell at a time, in all nine years.

Against the surveyors' own tilstand labels the scores come out in the right order: god highest, then moderat, then dårlig, then sværtRedusert lowest. The moderat class never enters a reference set, so its position was not fitted and is a genuine out-of-sample check.

Some of the national figure is the model recognising where a stand is rather than what condition it is in. What is being measured

AlphaEarth gives 64 numbers per cell per year, which is too many to reason about. The pipeline reduces them to one: the position along a line drawn from the average converted-land cell to the average good-condition cell. Call that line the naturalness axis. Step 1 asks how a cell has moved along it since 2017. Step 2 asks where it sits on it now.

Because both steps use the same line, a stand can be described by two independent facts — its level and its direction — without stitching together two unrelated methods.

The words used throughout this page.
TermWhat it means
condition scoreA number from 0 to 1. How close a cell sits to good-condition reference stands, set against how far it sits from converted land. Higher is better.
thresholdThe score above which a stand counts as being in good condition. It is fitted separately for each locality, leaving that locality's own stands out of the fit, so each stand has its own threshold and a single national cutoff is not correct.
separationThe chance that a randomly chosen good-labelled stand scores above a randomly chosen poor-labelled one. 0.50 is a coin flip, 1.00 is perfect. Statisticians call it AUC.
trajectoryThe direction of the nine-year series: improving, stable, fluctuating or declining.
localityA cluster of stands within 5 km of each other. There are of them. They are used to keep a stand from being compared with its own neighbours.
local / globalWhich good-condition stands are used as the reference: the nearest ones of the same forest type (local), or all of them (global). Both exclude any reference stand within 3 km of the cell being scored.

Step 1 · Which direction a stand has moved

Each stand and each cell gives nine annual positions on the naturalness axis. A straight line is fitted through them (a Theil–Sen slope, which ignores one-off outliers), and the stand is classified by how steep that line is — not by whether the trend passes a significance test.

That choice matters. A significance test asks whether a trend exists; on nine annual points it will call a tiny but tidy decline significant and a large but noisy one not. The steepness gate is set at the 2nd and 98th percentile of slopes measured on ground that Hansen Global Forest Change never recorded as cleared, so each class carries a 2 % false-alarm rate by construction. Step 2 · Where the stand has arrived

Every labelled stand is scored in the year it was actually surveyed. Scoring a stand labelled god in 2017 against its 2025 embedding would test what happened in the eight years since, not the method.

The first check is ordering: the median score should fall from god to sværtRedusert. It does, under both reference scopes, and the moderat class lands in between without ever having been used as a reference.

The score is a ratio, and both halves matter

A cell scores well by being close to good-condition stands and far from converted land. Neither half should be read on its own. Measured separately, distance from converted land separates the labels at and closeness to good stands at (local) or (global). The combined score beats both, at and per cell. Both distances ship as their own columns so this stays checkable.

Local and global reference scopes

The score depends on which good-condition stands it is measured against, and the pipeline computes it two ways. Global uses every god stand of the same forest type in the country. Local uses only the nearest eight. Both refuse any reference stand within 3 km of the cell being scored, so a stand is never compared with its own surroundings.

Neither is simply better, and both ship. The three figures below are the whole comparison.

Which to use. The disagreement runs almost entirely one way: stands called poor under the local scope but good under the global one, and essentially none the other way. That is the expected direction — a stand can look poor beside its immediate neighbours and ordinary by national standards. Use global when a stand has to be comparable with the rest of the country, which is also the only scope that covers all stands. Use local when the question is about this stand against its neighbours, and expect roughly three times as many stands to be flagged. The GeoPackage carries both.

What the separation survives

The numbers so far pool stands from all over the country. That lets a score score well simply by telling places apart, which is not the question anyone is asking. The question a manager asks is inside one place: of the stands here, which are degraded?

Two controls test that. The first recomputes the separation inside each 5 km locality. The second recomputes it inside groups of similar-sized stands, because the poor-labelled stands are systematically smaller, and a small stand is mostly edge — its cells are mixtures of the stand and whatever is next to it. Stand area is put through exactly the same tests as a baseline.

One problem no amount of statistics removes. All poor-labelled stands come from the MI survey layers; the NiN5k stands are all labelled god. So part of any good-versus-poor contrast is a contrast between two survey protocols. Only new field data can separate those.

What this run found

Because both steps measure along the same axis, the useful output is the combination. Agreement between them is reassuring; disagreement is where the management questions are. A stand still in good condition but losing ground is a priority. A stand in poor condition but recovering probably needs to be left alone.

Limits

  • Inside one locality, and controlling for stand size, the score is only a little better than chance. to on comparisons. This governs every other number on this page.
  • The within-locality evidence is localities, and one of them dominates. Only of localities contain a poor label at all. Nothing here is established across places, and the ranking of methods changes between them.
  • There are only poor-labelled stands. That is the binding constraint on the threshold, on every interval, and on any attempt to put a calibrated error rate on a single stand's class.
  • Every poor label comes from one survey protocol. See the note above.
  • Step 2 has no fully independent check. moderat is held out of the reference sets, but it comes from the same survey campaign and the same protocol as the labels that define them. Step 1 does have one, in Hansen.
  • Cells near a stand boundary are mixtures. Below 20 m from the edge a cell contains the stand and its surroundings in unknown proportion. The edge_m column is there so these can be excluded; many small stands have no cells that survive that filter.
  • Tested and not shipped. Eleven alternative definitions of "converted land" for the far end of the ratio, and the Global Human Modification layer as a forestry indicator. The first is a genuinely open question — some alternatives separate better within a locality; the second was rejected because its forestry band is effectively zero across Fennoscandia. Both are written up in docs/METHODS.md and research/.

condition_results.gpkg — the single file

Layer condition_pixels holds every scored 10 m cell as a square, with the same twenty measurements the rasters carry. Layer condition_stands holds the stands with their verdict, their interval and their nine-year series. A readme table inside the file explains every column, and both layers carry a default QGIS style, so dragging the file into QGIS is enough.

Why it is vector and not one large raster. The stands are spread over most of Norway across five UTM zones. A single raster has to choose one projection, and moving a 10 m grid between zones means resampling — the number in the cell would no longer be the number that was scored. Reprojecting a square resamples nothing: the cell becomes a slightly rotated quadrilateral over the same ground, carrying the same value. A national raster would also be wasteful: it would need 12.2 billion cells to carry real ones. Everything is in ETRS89 / UTM 33N (EPSG:25833).

The columns worth knowing. The rest are documented in the file's own readme table.

Three things to do before drawing conclusions from the map. Compare dor_global_2025 against threshold_global and not against a fixed value. Exclude cells with edge_m below 20 m when looking at small stands. Read m_good_global_2025 beside the score, because the score can rise either by being close to good stands or by being far from converted land.
